
Each of the once-through transition scenarios are compared on multiple 
criteria: the energy supplied, the number of advanced reactors deployed, 
the mass of enriched 
uranium required, the amount of \gls{SWU} capacity required to enrich uranium,
and the mass of waste produced. Each of these metrics can be used to inform 
policies and decisions of potential deployment schedules and the 
fuel cycle infrastructure required to support the deployment schedule. 

A subset of these results for similar scenario definitions is presented
in \cite{bachmann_enrichment_2021}, except the \glspl{LWR} were assumed to 
operate for 60 years if not closed before December 2020. This difference in 
the assumed lifetime of the \glspl{LWR} impacts the energy demand, 
the deployment schedule of advanced reactors, and the material 
requirements of the advanced reactors. The results of Scenario 
1 are presented first, followed by the results of the no growth scenarios 
(Scenarios 2-7), then the results of the 1\% growth scenarios (Scenarios 8-13)
for each metric. 

\section{Scenario 1}
Scenario 1 models only the \glspl{LWR} deploying the United States with no 
prescribed energy demand. This scenario provides insight on historical 
requirements of the nuclear indsutry as well as future demands if no 
new reactors are deployed and current reactors are decomissioned on 
this schedule. Because this scenario only includes the \glspl{LWR} and the 
deployment and material requirements of these reactors will be the same 
for each of the other scenarios (Scenarios 2-13), analysis 
of the other scenarios will focus on only the requirements of 
the advanced reactors deployed. Therefore, the results of Scenario 2-13 can 
be compared to the results of Scenario 1 to provide a comparison 
of material requirements between new and current reactors. 

Figure \ref{fig:energy_reactor1} shows the number of 
reactors deployed in Scenario 1 as a function of time compared to the 
energy produced by the \glspl{LWR} each year. The engery produced by the 
\glspl{LWR} follows with the number of reactors that are deployed, as 
one would expect. A maximum of 109 
\glspl{LWR} are deployed at one time in this scenario, producing 
between 101.24-102.46 GWe-yr, and 92 \glspl{LWR}
are deployed when the transition begins in 2025. The first \gls{LWR} is 
commissioned in August 1967, and all of the \glspl{LWR} are
decommissioned by October of 2055. In 2025, the \glspl{LWR} produce 
89.45 GWe-y of energy, which is used to define the energy demand for 
the other scenarios.

\subsection{No growth scenarios}

89.456 GWe-yr, based on the energy supplied by the reactors in Scenario 1
in 2025. The energy supplied in Scenarios 2-7 does not fully meet the demand
throughout the simulations (Figure \ref{fig:nogrowth_energy}). All of the 
transition scenarios experience the same energy production until 2030,
reaching a low point of 83.94 GWe-yr. Each of the scenrios exhibit oscillation 
in the annual energy production. This oscilaltion is a result of refueling 
time for the \glspl{LWR} and any VOYGRs present as well as the step 
between reactors being decommissioned and new ones being deployed. 

Throughout 
most of the time with advanced reactors, Scenario 5 has the lowest energy 
production, producing between 83.94-86.88 GWe-yr. Scenario 4 is able 
to best meet the demand, producing at least 89.35 GWe-yr starting in 2056.
Because each scenario experiences times of not fully meeting the energy 
demand, one can assume that the material requirements reported for each 
scenario are an underestimate and that additional reactors and materials
would be needed to fully meet the energy demand of the scenario.

\section{Reactor deployment}
The scenarios are compared on the number of advanced reactors that must 
be built to meet the prescribed energy demand. This metric takes two 
different forms: the number of reactors that are in the simulation at 
one time, and the number of reactors that are built (enter the simulation)
in a single time step. These metrics combined provide insight into the 
number of reactors that are needed and the rate at which they are needed. 

The \Cycamore \texttt{ManagerInst} institution archetype preferentially
deploys prototypes based on 
the order in which they are listed in the input file. The first prototype 
is deployed until deploying more would create a surplus of the required 
commodity (in this case power), then the next prototype is deployed until 
its deployment would create a surplus of the requried commodity, and so forth. 
Because of this scheme, the prototypes were listed in decreasing order of power 
output (i.e. Xe-100, VOYGR, then \gls{MMR}, for the necessary prototypes 
in that scenario) so that the number of reactors deployed is optimized to 
be the fewest reactors required for that scenario while also minimizing the 
oversupply of power. This ordering was used because it would most closely 
match a decision making process that would be implemented. 

\subsection{No growth scenarios}
Advanced reactors are first deployed in February 2030 for all scenarios, about 
5 years after transition start time. The delay between the start of the 
transition and the deployment of advanced reactors causes the deficit between 
energy demand and the energy produced in the transition scenarios. 
This initial deployment time is 
19 months earlier than the initial deployment time reported in 
\cite{bachmann_enrichment_2021}. The inital deployment time for advanced 
reactors for no growth transitions is only one month later than the 
initial deployment time reported in \cite{bachmann_enrichment_2021}
for 1\% growth transitions. 

The number of reactors that are deployed varies based on 
the type of reactor used in the scenario and scales with the power output of 
each type of reactor, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:nogrowth_reactors}. Scenario
2 requires the most reactors, because the \gls{MMR} has the smallest power 
output of the reactors considered in this work. Additionally, 8,946 
\gls{MMR} are deployed 
at one time in Scenario 2 (Table \ref{tab:reactors_nogrowth}) to meet the 
power demand. Scenario 3 requires the smallest number of advanced reactors 
because only the Xe-100 is deployed and this reactor design has the 
largest power output. 

Scenario 2 shows variation in the number of reactors that deployed 
after 2050. The variation is a result of the shorter lifetime of the 
\gls{MMR} compared with the Xe-100 and VOYGR. The shorter lifetime 
means that the \glspl{MMR} must be decomissioned and comissioned more 
frequently than the other reactors. The variation observed in Scenario 2 
occurs on 20 year cycles. Similar variations are observed in 
Scenarios 4 and 7, which also deploy the \gls{MMR}, although the variation 
in these scenarios is less pronounced than in Scenario 2 because other 
reactors are deployed as well. 

More reactors are initially deployed in Scenario 7 than in Scenario 4, 
but in March 2064 the number of advanced reactors in Scenario 7 decreases 
to less than what is deployed in Scenario 4. This occurs because the 
number of VOYGRs deployed in Scenario 7 increases and the number of 
\glspl{MMR} deployed decreases. The VOYGR has a larger ower output than 
the \gls{MMR} so fewer VOYGRs are required than \glspl{MMR} to meet the 
same power demand.

\subsection{1\% growth scenarios}
Advanced reactors are deployed starting in September 2026, which is more 
than three years before their initial deployment for a 1\% growth 
transition as reported in \cite{bachmann_enrichment_2021}. Advanced 
reactors in these scenarios are deployed 38 months (more than 3 years) 
earlier than for the no growth scenarios. Despite this earlier deployment, 
there is still the gap between the energy supplied and the demand 
(Figure \ref{fig:1percent_energy}). This suggests that to meet a 1\% 
growth in demand advanced reactors would need to be deployed even earlier, 
possibly even before 2025. 

The number of reactors deployed in these scenarios is similar to the 
trends observed for the no growth scenarios. Transitioning to only the 
\gls{MMR} (Scenario 8) requires the largest number of reactors, and 
transitioning to only the Xe-100 (Scenario 9) requires the fewest 
number of advanced reactors. Scenarios 10 and 13 have similar numbers of 
reactors deployed, as do Scenarios 9 and 12. 

Regarding the rate of reacotr deployemnt, Scenario 2 requires the most 
reactors to be deployed in a single time step (Table 
\ref{tab:reactors_added_1percent}), with 412 \glspl{MMR} deployed at once. 
Scenario 11 requires the next largest number of reactors deployed in 
a single time step, 77 VOYGRs in one step and 79 total advanced reactors. 
The other scenarios all have comparable numbers, around 50 reactors 
deployed at once.

\section{Uranium resources}
For the purposes of the transition scenarios, uranium resources refers to 
both the mass of enriched uranium and the mass of natural uranium feed 
required at each time step in the simulation. The feed uranium masses 
considered are the masses that would be needed at an enrichment facility,
and does not account for any processes losses between mining and 
enrichment. Therefore, the values reported are not the same as what would 
need to be mined. The masses of uranium resources are divided into four 
different metrics: the average monthly mass of uranium to supply all 
advanced reactors, the average monthly mass of \gls{HALEU} to supply 
advanced reactors, the maximum mass of uranium required by all advanced 
reactors in a single time step, and the cumulative mass required by the 
scenario. Each of these metrics provide information 
on how facilities can be designed to meet average and peak demands for 
uranium. 

\subsection{No growth scenarios}
The mass of enriched uranium sent to reactors (Fig. \ref{fig:nogrowth_uranium})
varies greatly based on the advanced reactors deployed in the scenario. 
Scenario 5 has the largest average mass of enriched uranium sent to 
reactors each month, followed by Scenario 2 (Table 
\ref{tab:nogrowth_uranium}). The larger averages for these scenarios is 
because of the large number of reactors deployed in Scenario 2 and 
because the VOYGR (deployed in Scenario 5) has the largest core mass.
Scenarios 2 and 5 also require the largest mass of enriched uranium sent 
to advanced reactors at one time. 

The largest average mass of \gls{HALEU} is required in Scenario 2, because 
of the large number of \glspl{MMR} deployed in this scenario. Scenario 
5 requires the least average mass of \gls{HALEU}, despite requiring the 
larges average mass of enriched uranium. This result is because most of the 
reactors in Scenario 5 are VOYGRs, which do not require \gls{HALEU}. 
Therefore only a small percent of the advanced reactors in Scenario 
5 require \gls{HALEU}, which reduced the demand for it. The deployment 
of VOYGRs in tandum with Xe-100's also results in a decrease in the mass 
of \gls{HALEU} required, but not to the same magnitude. The smaller 
magnitude decrease is because in Scenario 6 the Xe-100s are 
preferentially deployed over the VOYGRs, because the Xe-100s have a 
larger power output. Therefore, most of the reactors deployed in 
Scenario 6 are Xe-100s, which require \gls{HALEU}. 

Scenarios 2 and 5 require the largest mass of enriched uranium at a single 
time step. For Scenario 2, the large difference between the average and 
maximum amount of uranium is because of the refuleing scheme for the 
\gls{MMR}. The \gls{MMR} only requires fuel when it is first being 
commissioned, therefore there are periods in which no uranium is 
required by the reactors. In Scenario 5, 958.5 MTU (99.6\%) of the maximum 
enriched uranium 
mass is non-\gls{HALEU} for the VOYGRs. The large difference between the 
monthly average and the maximum presents challenges in designing facilities 
to meet the demand. Designing such a facility would have to consider the 
material demands while also limiting the amount of stockpiled enriched 
uranium at the facility, because this is considered a proliferation risk.

a lower mass of feed material required. Scenario 5 still requires more feed 
material than the other scenarios because the average product mass required 
is much larger than the other scenarios, which offsets some of the decrease 
in feed material from the decrease in the product assay. The other scenarios
(Scenarios 3, 4, 6, and 7) require similar average masses of feed material, 
at about 1000-1100 MT/month. 

When comparing the feed material to create \gls{HALEU} in each scenario, 
Scenarios 2-4 require the most feed material. This matches expectations 
because these scenarios deploy only reactors that require \gls{HALEU}, 
therefore all feed material is used to produce \gls{HALEU}. Scenario 5 
requires the least feed material to produce \gls{HALEU} because the vast 
majority of 
the reactors in this scenario are VOYGRs and do not require \gls{HALEU}.
Scenarios 6 and 7 require similar feed uranium masses (about 900 MTU/month)
despite Scenario 6 deploying a few hundred reactors fewer than Scenario 7. 
Scenario 7 deploys \glspl{MMR} in addition to the Xe-100s and VOYGRs,
which leads to the additional reactors in Scenario 7 and the decrease in 
the average feed material required because of the periods of no fuel being 
sent to the \glspl{MMR}. 

The maximum feed uranium required by each scenario poses some 
challenge in facility design. The maximum feed uranium required varies 
between 2.7-11.4 times larger than the average feed material, which means that 
facilities to produce feed uranium will likely need to be designed with a 
larger capacity than the average feed uranium required to meet the peaks. 
The cumulative mass of feed uranium required by each scenario is between 
800,000-860,000 MTU for every scenario except Scenarios 2 and 5. 
Scenario 2 requires over 1.2 million MT and 
Scenario 5 requires almost 950,000 MT of feed uranium. The \gls{EIA} reports 
about 176,000 MT of U$_3$O$_8$ reserves in the US available at up to 
\$100/pound at the end of 2019 \cite{us_eia_2020_2021}, which is about 
149,000 MT of uranium. Therefore, to meet the cumulative demand of feed 
uranium for each of these scenarios either uranium from international 
sources or uranium reserves at increased prices must be used.

\section{SWU capacity}
\subsection{No growth scenarios}
The \gls{SWU} capacity required to produce the enriched uranium for 
the no growth scenarios follows a similar pattern to what was observed for 
the feed uranium required by each scenario (Figure \ref{fig:nogrowth_swu}, 
Table \ref{tab:nogrowth_swu}). Scenario 2 requires the largest monthly 
average capacity, average capcity to produce \gls{HALEU}, and maximum 
capcaity needed. Scenario 5 requires the smallest averages for the total 
\gls{SWU} capacity and \gls{SWU} capacity to produce \gls{HALEU}. This 
result is because most of the enriched uranium in Scenario 5 is for the 
VOYGRs, which require the smallest product assay of the advanced reactors, 
despite the scenario requiring the highest average mass of enriched 
uranium for the no growth scenarios. Therefore for this scenario, the 
smaller product assay is enough to overcome the increase in the product mass
to result in a decrease in the \gls{SWU} capacity needed (Eq. \ref{eq:swu}).

The \gls{SWU} capacity required to produce \gls{HALEU} decreases when 
the VOYGR is deployed along with the other reactors, which is to be expected 
given that the VOYGR does not require \gls{HALEU}. With the exception 
of Scenario 5, Scenario 7 requires the least \gls{SWU} capacity to produce 
\gls{HALEU}. This result arises because this scenario deploys more 
Xe-100s than the other reactor types and although the Xe-100 requires 
the largest product assay, it also requires the smallest average mass of 
enriched uranium (see Table \ref{tab:nogrowth_uranium}). Therefore, the 
decrease in the product mass offsets the increase in the product 
assay (Eq. \ref{eq:swu}).

The \gls{SWU} capacity required to meet enriched uranium demand 
varies between 2.7-11.3 times the monthly average for all \gls{SWU}. 
The magnitude differences the average and the maximum pose potential 
problems for facility design and nonproliferation safeguards. Facilities 
will likely need to be built at a capacity greater than the average 
monthly requirement. Such a design means that there may be idle enrichment 
capacity, which is considered a proliferation risk.

\section{Waste}
The final metric of interest for the once-through transition scenarios is 
the amount of \gls{SNF} waste that must be disposed of. 
The amount of waste produced in a \gls{NFC} has implications on the options 
to treat and store the waste. In a once-through fuel cycle, all of the 
waste that is discharged from the reactors will be disposed of, although 
the timing is affected by the duration in wet storage. The waste masses 
reported here are based on the masses discharged from the reactor, which 
matches the capacity needs for disposal in a dry storage system or a 
geologic repository, but not necessarily the timing of the disposal. The 
disposal of \gls{HALEU} and non\gls{HALEU} fuel will likely take similar 
forms, so there is no separation between the two in this work. The masses 
presented here are the masses of all materials in the \gls{SNF}, not just 
the uranium discharged. This method of reporting was selected because in 
a once through fuel cycle, all materials in the \gls{SNF} must be disposed 
of, not just the uranium. 

For reference, Yucca Mountain has a capacity of about 63,000 MTIHM 
\cite{stahala_investigation_2008}, with a legal limit of 70,000 MTHM 
\cite{stahala_investigation_2008} with suggestions of increasing the 
capacity to 260,000 MTHU \cite{noauthor_yucca_nodate}. Capacity 
depends mostly on thermal limits of the facility, not space. 

\subsection{No growth scenarios}
The magnitude of discharged from the advanced reactors 
(Figure \ref{fig:nogrowth_waste}) follows the same pattern as the enriched 
uranium masses, but with different timing. This pattern matches expectations, 
because all of the material that enters the reactors are discharged in this 
\gls{NFC}. Scenario 5 experiences the largest average mass of \gls{SNF} 
discharged, but the smallest average mass of \gls{HALEU} discharged because 
most of the reactors in the scenario are VOYGRs which do not require 
\gls{HALEU} and require the largest material mass per core. 
The average mass of \gls{SNF} discharged by advanced reactors is less than 
the average discharged in Scenario 1, except Scenario 5. This result 
suggests how \gls{HALEU}-fueled reactors decrease the amount of \gls{SNF}
to be disposed of in a once-through fuel cycle. 

The first \gls{SNF} discharged from advanced reactors is in August 
2030 for Scenarios 3, 4, 6, and 7, in February 2032 in Scenario 5, and 
February 2050 in Scenario 2. The differences in timing of first \gls{SNF} 
discharge from advanced reactors is a result of the refueling scheme of 
each type of reactor. The Xe-100, which is deployed in Scenarios 3, 4, 6, 
and 7 utilizes online refueling, and is modeled as discharging \gls{SNF} 
every 6 months, which results in the first discharge soon after they 
are deployed. The VOYGR, deployed in Scenarios 4, 5, 6, and 7 has an 
18-month refueling cycle, so the first \gls{SNF} discharge for these 
reactors occurs 18 months after deployement. The \gls{MMR}, deployed in 
Scenarios 2, 4, 5, and 7, does not undergo refueling, so the first \gls{SNF} 
discharge occurs 20 years after first deployment. The first \gls{SNF} 
discharge for a scenario is governed by the shortest refueling time of 
the reactors deployed. Thus, the Xe-100 is the governing reactor for 
Scenarios 3, 4, 6, and 7, the VOYGR is the governing reactor for Scenario 
5, and the \gls{MMR} is the governing reactor for Scenario 2 with regards 
to \gls{SNF} dishcarge times. 

The cumulative waste masses for these scenarios are all of similar magnitude, 
except Scenario 5. The advanced reactors in all of the scenarios discharge 
less \gls{SNF} than the \glspl{LWR}. The cumulative mass of discharged 
\gls{SNF} for all of the no growth scenarios except Scenario 5 could be 
disposed of in a geologic repository cited in the US, which is required 
to have a capacity of at lest 70,000 MTHM \cite{noauthor_nuclear_1983}.
Most of the scenraios experience a consistent increase in the 
cumulative mass disharged (Figure \ref{fig:nogrowth_waste_cumulative}), 
except Scenraio 2. The waste discharged from Scenario 2, similar 
to the enriched urnaium mass, experiences peaks and valleys in the mass 
discharged, which results in variations in the increase in the 
cumulative mass discharged. Scenario 2 surpasses Scenarios 3 and 4 in 
the cumulative mass of discharged \gls{SNF} is because the \gls{MMR} 
core requires a greater mass of material than the Xe-100, which is the 
primary reactor deployed in Scenarios 3 and 4.
